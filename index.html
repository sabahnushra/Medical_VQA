THE PROBLEM

Getting the answer right is only half the story.

Medical vision–language models can sound convincing even when an image does not show what a question asks about. That is a different failure from giving an incorrect answer to a valid question.

We investigate image-grounded answerability: when should a model respond, and when should it acknowledge that the image alone is not enough?

!

The key distinction: response accuracy measures whether a model gives the right answer; answerability awareness measures whether it should answer the question in the first place.

OUR APPROACH

Filter, align, then decide.

Instead of retraining a medical language model, we supply an explicit image–question alignment signal through a small learned projection.

01

Route the question

A DistilBERT-based guardrail separates questions needing visual evidence from general, non-visual medical questions. Only image-grounded questions enter the answerability pathway.

02

Make the query alignable

Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct reformulates the question as a declarative visual statement, matching the caption-like text distribution used to pretrain BioMedCLIP.

03

Inject an abstention prior

Element-wise multiplication of normalized BioMedCLIP image and text embeddings produces a 512-dimensional correlation vector, mapped to LLaVA-Med's 4,096-dimensional feature space with a single linear layer.

EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

Much better at knowing when to abstain.

The combined projection is trained across all five domains. Below, Right Abstention (RA) measures the percentage of unanswerable questions correctly rejected; higher is better.

How do we score abstention?

We distinguish Correct Answers (CA) from Wrong Abstentions (WA) on answerable questions, and Right Abstentions (RA) from failures to abstain on unanswerable ones. A model should maximize CA and RA without rejecting legitimate questions unnecessarily.

WHAT THE EXPERIMENTS REVEAL

Answerability is not the same as answer accuracy.

01

Domain-specific features don't transfer cleanly

Projection layers trained on a single modality are often unreliable on other modalities. Even for the same answerability task, BioMedCLIP correlations have distinct domain distributions.

02

Stronger VLMs can still over-answer

General-purpose VLMs generally obtain higher correct-answer scores, but their right-abstention rates reach only 49.2% at best in the reported evaluation.

03

Failure cases have recognizable patterns

The combined model fails to abstain on 362 of 4,596 unanswerable test questions, often involving anatomy, pathology identification, and spatial localization.

Looking inside the alignment space

t-SNE of the 512-dimensional BioMedCLIP correlation signal

The clusters explain why a projection trained with one modality's alignment signal need not generalize to another. Joint training exposes the model to a wider signal distribution.

What the remaining failures look like

Of 4,596 unanswerable test pairs, 362 produced a response instead of abstention. Common words in those failures include mass, tumor, lesion, where, and located—suggesting difficulty when the question asks for a pathological finding or an anatomical position that is not established by the image.

LIMITATIONS & NEXT STEPS

What still needs to improve.

Constructed negative pairs are generated automatically from different images. Although a random subset was inspected, not every pair has been manually verified; a few transplanted questions may still be answerable from their new image. The evaluation also relies on language models for some filtering and judging decisions, and the images come from biomedical publications rather than necessarily representing raw clinical scans.

We evaluate the learned projection with LLaVA-Med. The adaptation raises appropriate abstention substantially, but can also reject some valid questions. Future work should improve fine-grained anatomical alignment, test transfer to other medical VLMs, and better balance right abstention against unnecessary refusal.

→

The direction forward: improve the alignment signal so models can identify unsupported anatomy or localization claims while preserving accurate answers to genuinely answerable questions.
